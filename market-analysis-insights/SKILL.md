# Market Analysis & Insights

## Purpose

Turn fragmented market information into decision-useful commercial insights. Do not stop at describing facts. Explain:

1. What is happening in the market?
2. Why is it happening?
3. Which customers, competitors, channels, and external forces matter?
4. What does it mean for the business?
5. What should the business do next, and how will results be measured?

Use the reasoning chain:

**Evidence → Interpretation → Insight → Business implication → Recommended action → KPI**

## When to use this skill

Use when the user asks to:
- Research a market, category, country, or region.
- Analyze customers, consumer needs, reviews, or purchase behavior.
- Map competitors, compare brands or products, or assess positioning.
- Evaluate pricing, promotions, retailers, marketplaces, dealers, or DTC channels.
- Assess market entry, product-market fit, go-to-market strategy, or growth opportunities.
- Turn an existing report, HTML page, spreadsheet, presentation, or dataset into strategic insights.
- Prepare an executive or management-ready market analysis.

## Three analytical lenses

### 1. Consumer Intelligence

Understand demand from the customer's perspective:
- Jobs to be done and core use cases.
- Customer segments and practical personas.
- Purchase triggers, desired outcomes, and decision criteria.
- Pain points, objections, perceived risks, and unmet needs.
- Customer journey: awareness, consideration, evaluation, purchase, ownership, and advocacy.
- Voice of Customer (VOC): reviews, forums, interviews, surveys, support records, and social discussion.
- Messaging that addresses real customer motivations and concerns.

Treat reviews and online discussions as directional qualitative evidence, not automatically representative survey data. Separate recurring themes from isolated anecdotes.

### 2. Market & Positioning Intelligence

Understand the market structure and the competitive choices:
- Market size, growth, adoption, maturity, and category boundaries.
- Relevant segments by customer, use case, price tier, product type, and geography.
- Competitor groups: direct, indirect, price-led, technology-led, premium, and channel competitors.
- Brand promise, target audience, product portfolio, price architecture, differentiators, and proof points.
- Product-level comparisons using genuinely comparable specifications and configurations.
- Positioning gaps, white spaces, barriers to entry, and likely competitive responses.

Distinguish brand owner, manufacturer, importer, distributor, retailer, and marketplace seller. Verify relationships from reliable sources rather than inferring them from product listings.

### 3. Commercial & Channel Intelligence

Assess how demand is converted into revenue:
- Price architecture: list price, street price, promotional price, bundles, and financing.
- Channel structure: brand DTC, marketplaces, specialist retailers, dealers, distributors, and omnichannel.
- Channel roles, customer reach, assortment, availability, service, fulfilment, returns, and after-sales support.
- Retail merchandising, product content, reviews, search visibility, and conversion friction.
- Promotion calendar, discount depth, campaign mechanics, and channel conflict.
- Where data permits: gross margin, contribution, customer acquisition cost, conversion, sell-through, stock cover, and return rates.

Do not assume that a brand's presence at a retailer proves exclusivity, ownership, market leadership, or high sales.

## Working principles

### Start with the decision

Before researching, identify the business decision the analysis should support. Examples:
- Should the company enter this market?
- Which customer segment should be prioritized?
- What product and price architecture should be launched?
- Which competitors should be benchmarked?
- Which channel should receive investment?
- What should change in the current go-to-market plan?

If a critical input is missing, ask one concise clarification. Otherwise proceed with explicit assumptions and label them.

### Evidence hierarchy

Prioritize evidence in this order, while considering relevance and recency:

1. Government statistics, regulators, customs/trade data, legislation, and official datasets.
2. Official company, brand, retailer, and product documentation.
3. Industry associations, recognized research institutions, and credible market studies.
4. Reputable business and trade publications.
5. Retail listings, price trackers, and marketplace observations.
6. Customer reviews, forums, social media, and other qualitative sources.

Use lower-tier evidence when it is the best available source, but label its limitations.

### Evidence recording

For each important metric or claim, capture where possible:
- Metric and value.
- Unit and currency.
- Geography and category definition.
- Time period and publication date.
- Source name and direct URL.
- Evidence type: observed fact, third-party estimate, qualitative signal, assumption, or hypothesis.
- Confidence: High, Medium, or Low.

Keep source notes close to the relevant claim. Do not cite a homepage when a more specific source page is available.

### Never overstate the data

- Do not equate product/SKU counts with sales, market share, or popularity.
- Do not equate review counts with unit sales unless a defensible methodology supports the conversion.
- Do not infer exclusivity, distribution rights, ownership, or official partnership from a retailer listing alone.
- Do not combine figures with different category definitions, geographies, time periods, or units without explaining the difference.
- Do not present market estimates as official statistics.
- Do not invent missing numbers, quotes, sources, URLs, customer feedback, or KPIs.
- If sources conflict, show the range or explain the discrepancy rather than silently choosing one.
- State when evidence is insufficient to support a conclusion.

## Research workflow

### Step 1 — Define scope and decision

Record:
- Country/region.
- Product category and category boundaries.
- Time period.
- Target customer and use cases.
- Company/product context, if supplied.
- The decision to be made.
- Required output format and depth.

### Step 2 — Build the evidence base

Create a source plan across market, consumer, competitor, channel, pricing, regulation, and macroeconomic factors. Search for the most recent evidence for time-sensitive topics. Check the source's date, geography, methodology, and whether it directly supports the claim.

### Step 3 — Establish the market picture

Analyze:
- Market size and growth, including the definition and methodology behind each estimate.
- Adoption, penetration, and category maturity.
- Demand drivers and constraints.
- Customer and product segmentation.
- Seasonality and purchase cycles.
- Regulation, safety, standards, and other market-entry requirements.
- Regional differences where meaningful.

If market-size data is weak, use a transparent triangulation approach and label the result as an estimate, not a fact.

### Step 4 — Analyze customers

Build a concise set of evidence-based segments or personas. For each, describe:
- Context and job to be done.
- Purchase trigger.
- Desired benefits.
- Main decision criteria.
- Budget/price sensitivity, if evidenced.
- Main objections and perceived risks.
- Preferred discovery and purchase channels.
- Relevant product and messaging implications.

Use direct customer language sparingly and accurately. Do not manufacture quotes.

### Step 5 — Map competitors and positioning

Build a competitor matrix with relevant fields such as:
- Brand and market role.
- Target customer/use case.
- Representative products.
- Comparable price range and observed price date.
- Key specifications and differentiators.
- Warranty, service, and distribution footprint, when verified.
- Strengths, weaknesses, and evidence gaps.

Classify competitors by the threat or benchmark they represent. A brand can be a product benchmark without being a direct volume competitor.

For positioning, explain:
- Who the brand serves.
- Which need it solves.
- Which category or alternative it competes against.
- Its differentiated value.
- What evidence supports that promise.

### Step 6 — Analyze pricing and channels

Compare like with like. Normalize currency, tax treatment, configuration, accessories, delivery, warranty, and promotional status where possible.

For each relevant channel, assess:
- Role in discovery and conversion.
- Reach and customer fit.
- Assortment and availability.
- Price and promotional behavior.
- Content and merchandising quality.
- Fulfilment, returns, service, and repair.
- Economics and operational requirements, where data exists.

### Step 7 — Convert analysis into insights

An insight must go beyond a fact. Use this structure:

- **Evidence:** What has been observed?
- **Interpretation:** What does it indicate, and why?
- **Insight:** What non-obvious conclusion follows?
- **Business implication:** Why does it matter to this company?
- **Action:** What should be done?
- **KPI:** What observable measure would indicate progress?

Assign confidence:
- **High:** supported by multiple strong, consistent sources or reliable primary data.
- **Medium:** supported by credible but incomplete evidence.
- **Low:** plausible hypothesis with limited or indirect evidence.

Keep hypotheses visibly separate from established findings.

### Step 8 — Prioritize recommendations

Prioritize by expected impact, evidence confidence, urgency, cost, and execution complexity. Avoid false precision when inputs are missing.

A practical roadmap can use:
- 0–30 days: validate assumptions and close critical evidence gaps.
- 31–60 days: run focused product, pricing, channel, or messaging tests.
- 61–90 days: scale validated actions and review performance.

For each action specify owner, deliverable, timing, KPI, and dependencies when known. Do not invent numeric targets; propose target-setting only after a baseline is available.

## Standard report structure

Adapt depth to the task, but use this order for a full market analysis:

1. Executive summary — key conclusions and decisions required.
2. Market snapshot — size, growth, maturity, drivers, barriers, regulation.
3. Customer intelligence — segments, use cases, needs, objections, journey.
4. Competitive landscape — competitor groups, brand/product matrix, positioning.
5. Pricing and channel landscape — price architecture, channel roles, promotion.
6. Strategic insights — evidence-led implications and opportunity areas.
7. Recommendations — prioritized actions and trade-offs.
8. 30/60/90-day roadmap — owners, deliverables, KPIs, dependencies.
9. Sources, assumptions, and limitations.

Lead each section with the conclusion, then show the supporting evidence. Use tables for comparisons and concise prose for interpretation.

## Output standards

### Executive summary
- Lead with 3–7 decision-relevant findings.
- State what the findings mean for the business.
- Identify the most important opportunity, risk, and next action.
- Avoid generic statements such as “the market has potential” unless quantified and explained.

### HTML report
When asked for HTML:
- Deliver one self-contained HTML file unless the user asks otherwise.
- Embed CSS and JavaScript; avoid dependencies that require a build step.
- Use responsive layouts, clear navigation, readable charts/tables, and a print stylesheet.
- Include source notes and a limitations section.
- Ensure charts use traceable data and clearly labeled units/time periods.
- When updating an existing report, preserve its useful structure and content, and clearly integrate new findings rather than replacing unrelated sections.

### PowerPoint
- One key message per slide.
- Use insight-led slide titles, not generic labels.
- Keep charts and tables readable.
- Put source notes on the relevant slide and detailed methodology in an appendix.
- Separate facts, estimates, hypotheses, and recommendations.

### Excel
- Keep raw data separate from analysis and summary sheets.
- Preserve source, date, unit, currency, and geography fields.
- Make formulas transparent and avoid hard-coded derived values where practical.
- Include a definitions/methodology sheet for complex datasets.

## Quality-control checklist

Before delivery, verify:
- Does the analysis answer the stated business decision?
- Are facts, estimates, assumptions, hypotheses, and recommendations distinguishable?
- Do key claims have relevant sources and dates?
- Are geography, category definition, units, and currency consistent?
- Are competitor comparisons genuinely comparable?
- Have SKU counts, reviews, and retailer listings been kept separate from sales and market share?
- Are ownership, distribution, and exclusivity claims verified?
- Are customer-review themes presented with appropriate limitations?
- Are current regulations checked against official sources?
- Do recommendations follow from the evidence?
- Are KPIs measurable and not fabricated?
- Are limitations and unanswered questions explicit?

## Example application: Australian e-bike market

For an Australian e-bike study, the analysis may consider commuting, recreation, family/cargo, and low-effort mobility use cases; customer concerns such as real-world range, battery safety, reliability, repairability, warranty, and local service; and channel differences across specialist retailers, general sporting-goods retailers, marketplaces, and brand DTC.

Potential brands for investigation may include Pedal, Reid, NCM, Aventon, DiroDi, Cube, Merida, Giant, Trek, Specialized, and Riese & Müller. Treat this as an initial research universe, not a fixed ranking or a claim about market share. Verify current Australian availability, product specifications, prices, distribution relationships, and after-sales support from current sources.

A retailer such as 99 Bikes can be analyzed as a channel and portfolio case study. Its product assortment can reveal positioning and merchandising choices, but listings alone do not establish sell-through, exclusivity, or brand market share.

The Australian example is illustrative only. Rebuild the competitor set, customer segments, regulations, and channel map for each new market rather than reusing this list mechanically.
